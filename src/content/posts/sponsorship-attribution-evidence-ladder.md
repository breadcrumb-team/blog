**Sponsorship attribution** is the work of connecting a sponsorship to outcomes the sponsor can count. In a recap, not all evidence is equal. Impressions and media value are the weakest proof. Engagement is a little stronger. Surveys and brand lift studies are stronger again. Confirmed transactions, like redeemed offers, card linked spend, promo code sales, new accounts, and booked test drives, are the strongest proof you can put in front of a sponsor's finance team.

The short version: lead with the strongest rung you have, label every number with how it was measured, and never dress up a weak rung as a strong one.

This matters because most sponsors are still stuck. Forrester's [Q4 2024 B2C Marketing CMO Pulse Survey](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/sports-sponsorships-surge-despite-fuzzy-roi/) found that 76% of US B2C marketing executives who invested in sports sponsorships agree they struggle to calculate the ROI. Kantar [reported in 2023](https://www.kantar.com/north-america/inspiration/brands/determining-sponsorship-roi) that only 19% of sponsorship professionals are confident they can measure the business value return. And the [ANA and MASB](https://themasb.org/sponsorship-accountability-part-5-measurement/) found that just under 40% of major marketers have a standardized sponsorship measurement process, a share that has barely moved since 2010.

If you are a rights holder, that gap is your opening. The property that shows up with clean proof is the one that gets renewed.

## Why sponsorship attribution decides renewals

Your champion at the brand likes the partnership. Their CFO does not know your venue, your fans, or your content. The CFO sees a line item and asks one question: what did we get for this?

A recap that answers with "12 million impressions" invites a shrug. A recap that answers with "1,840 new checking accounts opened with the partner code, verified against the bank's own records" invites a renewal conversation.

You do not need perfect attribution to win. You need honest evidence, ranked clearly, so finance can see what is proven and what is directional.

## The sponsorship evidence ladder, weakest to strongest

| Rung | Evidence type | Examples | What finance thinks | How to report it so it holds up |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Exposure and media value | Impressions, broadcast minutes, logo seconds, media equivalency | "Nice, but we did not pay for eyeballs alone." | State the source, the counting method, and the valuation rate. Never present media value as revenue. |
| 2 | Engagement | Clicks, video views, booth visits, QR scans, contest entries, email signups | "People noticed. Did they do anything that matters?" | Report unique people, not raw actions. Remove bots and duplicates. Show cost per engaged person. |
| 3 | Surveys and brand lift | Sponsor recall, favorability, consideration, purchase intent | "Directional, if the method is sound." | Show sample size, exposed versus unexposed groups, the baseline, and the margin of error. |
| 4 | Confirmed transactions | Offer redemptions, card linked spend, promo code sales, accounts opened, test drives booked, first store visits | "Now we can do math." | Match against the sponsor's own system of record. Show the time window, the unique ID used, and a baseline or control. |

The [MASB sponsorship accountability work](https://themasb.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/ANA-MASB_Improving-Sponsorship-Accountability-Metrics.pdf) describes a similar journey, from media equivalency to return on objectives to financial attribution. The ladder above is the practical version for a recap deck.

## How to report each rung so a CFO accepts it

### Rung 1: impressions and media value

Keep it, but shrink it. Put exposure in a short section near the back, not the cover. Say where the number came from (broadcaster logs, a third party valuation vendor, your own social analytics) and what rate you used to value it. If your valuation assumes an ad buy the sponsor would never actually make, say so.

### Rung 2: engagement

Engagement is useful when it is tied to a real person. A QR scan that leads to an email signup is better than a like. Report unique engaged people, the action they took, and what happened next. If you can, show the funnel: scanned, signed up, redeemed.

### Rung 3: surveys and brand lift

A survey is only as good as its design. Finance will ask how many people answered, whether you compared fans who saw the activation with similar people who did not, and what the numbers looked like before the season. Put those details right next to the result. A 6 point lift with a clear method beats a 20 point lift with no method.

### Rung 4: confirmed transactions

This is the rung that turns a recap into a business case. The key is that the sponsor's own records confirm it, not yours alone. A unique offer code, a dedicated landing page, a card linked offer, or a test drive booking source tag gives the sponsor something to check in their own system. Always show:

1. The unique identifier that ties the outcome to the sponsorship.
2. The time window.
3. A baseline, a control group, or a comparison market, so finance can see what is incremental.
4. Who verified the count.

Then, and only then, translate it into value. Accounts times average first year value. Test drives times historical close rate times average margin. Show the math in one line so finance can redo it.

## What proof looks like by sponsor category

Different sponsors trust different outcomes. Build your recap around the rung four metric their CFO already tracks.

**Banks and credit unions.** They care about new accounts opened, new cards issued, and card spend at your venue or partner merchants. A partner specific signup link or a card linked offer lets the bank confirm accounts in its own core system. Concession and merch spend on the sponsor's card, compared with the prior season, is strong evidence too.

**Auto brands and dealer groups.** They care about test drives and leads that turn into sales. Use a booking link or a code that dealers capture in their CRM, then report booked test drives, completed test drives, and, where the dealer will share it, sold units within a set window. The [Nissan sponsorship case study](/blog/carjitsu-nissan-sponsorship-case-study/) shows what this kind of activation can look like.

**Local retail and restaurants.** They care about first visits and repeat visits. A fan offer redeemed at the register, tied to a unique code or loyalty account, shows new customers walking in. Report first time redeemers separately from existing customers, since new customers are what the owner is paying for.

**Consumer brands and beverage partners.** They care about sales lift in the market. Promo code redemptions, retailer scan data in your market versus a comparison market, and sampling followed by a trackable offer all help.

## A checklist before you send the recap

- [ ] The cover page leads with the strongest rung you have, not the biggest number.
- [ ] Every metric is labeled with its rung and its source.
- [ ] Exposure and media value are clearly marked as estimates, not revenue.
- [ ] Engagement counts unique people and removes duplicates.
- [ ] Survey results show sample size, method, baseline, and a comparison group.
- [ ] Transactions are matched to the sponsor's own records with a unique ID.
- [ ] Each transaction metric shows a time window and a baseline or control.
- [ ] Value math is shown in one line that finance can repeat.
- [ ] Gaps are named honestly, with a plan to close them next season.
- [ ] The recap ends with the measurement plan you propose for the renewal term.

## Plan attribution before the deal, not after the event

The strongest proof is designed in, not dug up later. Agree on the rung four metric during the deal. Write the unique code, the landing page, or the data share into the contract. Set the baseline before kickoff. The ANA study found only 40% of marketers write measurement expectations into contracts with properties, so a rights holder who brings a measurement plan stands out.
