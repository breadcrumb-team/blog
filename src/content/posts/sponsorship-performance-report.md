A **sponsorship performance report** is an ongoing, per partner view of what a sponsor bought, what you delivered, and what it earned. Unlike a one time recap, it updates through the season. Lead with the partner's goals, show delivery against the contract, add outcomes with sources, and flag gaps and upsells early.

This guide is for partnership services, activation, and account managers at teams, leagues, media companies, and creator networks. It is also for the VPs who own renewals and need reporting that does not eat their team's week.

## What is a sponsorship performance report?

A sponsorship performance report is a living document, one per partner, that tracks delivery and results across the whole term of a deal. It answers one question at any point in the season: how is this partnership doing right now?

That makes it different from two documents you probably already send. A [post event sponsorship report](/blog/post-event-sponsorship-report) covers one event or one moment, after it ends. A [sponsorship fulfillment report](/blog/sponsorship-fulfillment-report) proves you delivered the contracted assets.

The performance report sits on top of both. It pulls delivery, exposure, and outcomes into one view that stays current from the first week of the deal to the renewal meeting.

## Why a once a season recap is not enough anymore

A single recap at season end arrives too late to change anything. Your sponsor contact has to justify spend to their own finance team during the year, not only when the contract comes up. If they have nothing current to show, the partnership looks like a cost with no story.

Static decks have a few other problems:

* **They go stale.** By the time a deck is built and sent, the numbers are weeks old.
* **They are built by hand.** Someone pulls screenshots and exports from every platform, every time.
* **They look the same for every partner.** A bank and a sportsbook get the same slides with the logo swapped.
* **They cannot answer questions.** When the sponsor asks "which posts drove that?", you start another round of digging.

Pro League Network saw this firsthand. After moving to live reporting, weekly prep went from 4 to 6 hours to under 30 minutes, and reporting turnaround went from 3 to 5 days after the week ended to real time. You can read the details in the [Pro League Network case study](/blog/pro-league-network-social-analytics-case-study).

When reporting stops being a weekly chore, your team can spend that time on the partner instead of the spreadsheet.

## What to include in a sponsorship performance report

A good report has seven parts. Each one answers a question the sponsor is already asking.

### The partner's objectives, in their words

Start with what the sponsor said they wanted when they signed. Awareness, leads, product trial, hospitality, or some mix. Use their language, not yours.

Every number that follows should connect back to one of these goals. If a metric does not serve a stated objective, it probably belongs in an appendix.

### Contracted versus delivered

Next, show what was promised and where each item stands. A short status table works well:

| Asset | Contracted | Delivered so far | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sponsored social posts | 40 | 31 | On track |
| In venue signage nights | 12 | 12 | Delivered |
| Email newsletter features | 6 | 4 | Changed: two moved to playoffs |
| Hospitality suite nights | 4 | 2 | Missed one, make good scheduled |
| Bonus video segment | 0 | 1 | Over delivered |

*Illustrative example. Use your own contract line items.*

Four labels are enough: delivered, changed, missed, over delivered. For the full method of documenting delivery, see our guide to the [sponsorship fulfillment report](/blog/sponsorship-fulfillment-report).

### Exposure and engagement, by platform

Report impressions, views, and engagement rate for each platform. Put the source and the date range next to every number. A figure without a source is a figure finance will question.

Keep platforms separate rather than blending them into one total. Sponsors often care more about one channel than the others, and blended totals hide that.

### Value and outcomes

This is where you show what the exposure was worth and what it produced. Include earned media value with the method and range stated, plus any results the sponsor shares with you, like redemptions, signups, or test drives.

Not all evidence carries the same weight. Our [sponsorship attribution evidence ladder](/blog/sponsorship-attribution-evidence-ladder) explains what counts as proof, and our guide on [how to measure sponsorship ROI](/blog/how-to-measure-sponsorship-roi) covers the formulas. The performance report just needs to show the results clearly and label how each was measured.

### Where value concentrated

Point out which posts, moments, or assets did most of the work. Value is rarely spread evenly. Usually a small share of content drives a large share of results.

This section is often the most useful one for the sponsor, because it tells both of you what to do more of.

### Flags

Name anything that is behind, and pair it with a make good. Then name anything that is going unusually well and could justify more investment.

Raising underdelivery early builds trust. Raising upsell signals early gives the sponsor time to find budget.

### Next step

End with one recommendation tied to the data. Not a list of ideas, just one clear next move. "Shift two remaining posts to match day content, where engagement has been strongest" is better than "keep up the great work."

## How often to send it (and what changes each time)

The short answer: keep a live link always on, and add a summary, a review, and a renewal brief at set points. Here is a practical cadence that works for most portfolios.

* **Live link, always on.** The sponsor can check delivery and results whenever they want, without waiting for you.
* **Monthly or per flight summary.** A short note with three to five headline numbers, what changed since last time, and any flags.
* **Quarterly review conversation.** A call to walk through the report, revisit objectives, and agree on adjustments.
* **Renewal brief near the end of term.** A short case for what comes next, built on everything the report has collected.

Each touchpoint builds on the last, so the renewal brief is mostly already written by the time you need it. For more on that final conversation, see our piece on [sponsorship ROI and renewals](/blog/sports-sponsorship-roi-analytics-renewal).

## Make it per partner, not one template for everyone

Different sponsors care about different numbers, so each report should tell a different story on top of the same data. A bank wants account openings and trust. An automaker wants test drives and reach in the right markets. A sportsbook wants signups and activity on game days.

The data underneath can be identical. What changes is what you lead with, which metrics get the spotlight, and how you frame the results against that sponsor's objectives.

A practical test: write three insights for each partner that could not apply to any other sponsor in your portfolio. If you can swap the logo and the insight still works, it is not specific enough.

## Static template vs live report

A static template is a fine start, but a live report is easier to keep current and easier to personalize at scale. Here is how they compare.

| | Static template | Live report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it's built | Manual exports, screenshots, slides | Connected to the data you already have |
| Freshness | Accurate on the day it was made | Updates automatically |
| Personalization | Rebuilt by hand for each partner | Same data, different view per partner |
| Follow up questions | New round of digging each time | Sponsor can ask the data directly |
| Sharing | PDF or slide file attached to email | Public link, no login needed |
| Effort per partner | Grows with every sponsor you add | Stays roughly flat |

To be fair to templates: if you manage a handful of partners and report a few times a year, a well built template is perfectly reasonable. The case for live reporting grows with the size of your portfolio and how often sponsors ask for updates.

## Example: how CarJitsu reported the Nissan partnership

CarJitsu's Nissan Magnite Showdown shows what a performance report looks like when it is built around the partner's goals. Here is the scorecard logic, using the real figures from the [CarJitsu and Nissan case study](/blog/carjitsu-nissan-sponsorship-case-study).

**Delivery against goal.** The partnership delivered 135% of the impression goal and 103% of contracted posts. That is the first thing a sponsor wants to see: did we get what we paid for, and then some?

**Volume.** There were 141 Nissan tagged posts, generating 30.6M impressions, 29.2M video views, and 1.3M total engagements.

**Engagement against benchmark.** The engagement rate was 3.77%, compared with an industry benchmark of 1.5 to 3% as stated in the case study. Showing the benchmark next to the number gives it meaning.

**Value, with its range.** Estimated earned media value came to about $859K, within a range of $483K to $1.3M, estimated with industry media value benchmarking methods. Showing the range keeps the number honest and makes it easier for finance to accept.

**Where value concentrated.** Just 7% of posts generated 30% of impressions. That single insight tells the sponsor and the creator team exactly where to focus next time.

The report itself was live. It updated automatically, was shared by public link with no login, and let Nissan ask questions about the data in plain English. Pro League Network later extended the same approach to Ford Bronco Off Course and Optima MotoRodeo.

The partnership also won Most Creative Partnership at Hashtag Sports for the second year running. The report did not win that award, but it gave both sides a clear record of what the partnership delivered.

## A simple sponsorship performance report template

Use this outline as a starting point. Copy it, fill it in for one partner, and adjust the order to match what that sponsor cares about most.

1. **Cover.** Partner name, reporting period, and the date of the last data update.
2. **Summary.** Three to five headline numbers that matter most to this partner.
3. **Objectives.** The sponsor's goals, in their words.
4. **Contracted versus delivered.** Status table with delivered, changed, missed, and over delivered items.
5. **Exposure and engagement.** Impressions, views, and engagement rate by platform, each with source and date.
6. **Outcomes.** Earned media value with method and range, plus sponsor reported results.
7. **Where value concentrated.** The posts, moments, or assets that did the most work.
8. **Flags.** Underdelivery with make goods, and upsell signals.
9. **Next step.** One recommendation tied to the data.
10. **Sources.** Where every number came from, and when it was pulled.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between a sponsorship performance report and a sponsorship recap?

A recap covers one event or the end of a season, while a sponsorship performance report is ongoing and updates throughout the term. The recap looks back once. The performance report shows the sponsor where things stand at any point, so problems and opportunities surface while there is still time to act. For single events, see our guide to the [post event sponsorship report](/blog/post-event-sponsorship-report).

### What should a sponsorship performance report include?

It should include the partner's objectives, contracted versus delivered assets, exposure and engagement by platform, value and outcomes, where value concentrated, flags, and one next step. Every number should carry its source and date. Lead with what this specific sponsor cares about most, and keep anything that does not serve their goals out of the main view.

### How often should you send sponsors a performance report?

Keep a live link available at all times, then send a short monthly or per flight summary. Add a quarterly review conversation and a renewal brief near the end of the term. This is a practical rhythm rather than a fixed rule. Larger or more active partners may want more frequent check ins, smaller ones less.

### Who should own sponsor performance reporting on a partnerships team?

The account or partnership services manager who works with the sponsor day to day should own it. They know the sponsor's goals and history best. Data or marketing teams can supply the numbers, but the person closest to the relationship should decide what the report emphasizes and handle the conversation around it.

### How do you report underdelivery to a sponsor?

Report it early and pair it with a make good. Say what was missed, why, and what you will deliver instead, with a date. Sponsors handle a shortfall much better when they hear it from you mid season than when they discover it at renewal. Keep the explanation short and the fix specific.

### Should a sponsorship performance report include earned media value?

Yes, as long as you show the method and the range, and never present it alone. Earned media value is an estimate, so a single precise figure can look more certain than it is. Put it alongside delivery, engagement, and any outcomes the sponsor can verify, so it supports the story instead of carrying it.

### How do you personalize sponsor reports without building each one by hand?

Keep one shared data source and change the view for each partner. The underlying numbers stay the same, while each report leads with the metrics and objectives that sponsor cares about. Live reporting tools make this easier, because you set up each partner's view once and it stays current automatically.

### What is proof of performance in sponsorship?

Proof of performance is documented evidence that you delivered what the contract promised, such as posts, signage, and hospitality. It is the delivery layer of a performance report. For how to collect and present it, see our guide to the [sponsorship fulfillment report](/blog/sponsorship-fulfillment-report).
