Give sponsors fan data as aggregated, consented numbers, never as a list of fans. Report how many fans the partnership reached, who they were as shares and segments, and what they did, with each figure sourced. Fans consented to you, not the sponsor, so keep records in house and share insight.

This guide is for partnership insights leads, partner services managers and account leads at teams, leagues, media companies and event owners. Renewal recaps are being built right now, and more sponsors are asking a harder question than "how many people saw us?" They want to know who those people were. In [Dizplai's Anonymous Fan Index](https://dizplai.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/TheAnonymousFanIndex_report-v4.pdf), 87% of rights holders said they feel moderate to high pressure from sponsors for measurable fan engagement data, and 60% said at least a quarter of their renewals are tied to engagement or fan data.

This is not about your wider [fan engagement strategies](/blog/ai-fan-engagement-strategies-sports-marketing). It is about the narrower job of reporting your audience to a partner in a way that is credible, useful and safe.

## Why sponsors are asking for fan data now

Sponsors are buying proof of audience, not only reach, and renewals increasingly depend on it. A logo count tells a brand it was visible. An audience profile tells it whether it was visible to the right people.

The [Dizplai report](https://dizplai.com/insights/the-anonymous-fan-index/) puts numbers on that pressure. Beyond the 87% and 60% figures above, it found that 35% of underdog organisations tie more than half of their renewals to engagement. Worth knowing before you lean on these figures: the sample is 50 sports organisations, 24 of them rights holders. It is a useful signal, not a census.

The same research points to the gap you will have to manage. [Organisations know only about 24% of their fanbase](https://dizplai.com/our-thinking/report-finds-sport-organisations-only-know-24-of-their-fanbase/) by name and contact. So when you describe "the audience" to a sponsor, you are usually describing the part of it you can see. Your reporting has to be honest about that share, or the sponsor will eventually notice the gap for you.

## What fan data a sponsor should get (and what they should not)

Totals, shares and segments, yes. Names, emails, phone numbers, or anything that lets a sponsor pick out one fan, no.

The reason is simple. Fans gave their consent to the club, not to the sponsor, a point [Toutix makes in its sponsor recap guide](https://www.toutix.com/guides/sponsor-recap-report). Its [CRM guide for sports clubs](https://www.toutix.com/guides/crm-for-sports-clubs) adds that fan data can inform a partner report, but fans should not become the sponsor's sales leads.

| Share with the sponsor | Keep in house |
| --- | --- |
| Attendance and reach at sponsor events and moments | Individual fan records |
| Number of fans who used a sponsor offer or entered a promotion | Names, emails, phone numbers and addresses |
| Audience profile as percentages and bands | Raw transaction rows |
| Segment sizes, such as season ticket holders or families | Very small segments that could identify someone |
| Change over the season | Exact ages, postcodes or anything joined at person level |

If a sponsor pushes for the right column, treat it as a contract conversation, not a reporting favour.

## How to report fan reach in aggregate

Report four things: reach at sponsor moments, participation, audience profile and change over time. Give each metric a source and a date, the same habit you would bring to a [sponsorship performance report](/blog/sponsorship-performance-report).

### Reach at sponsor moments

Start with attendance and digital reach wherever the sponsor was present. Break it out by event, match or campaign flight, so the sponsor can see which moments carried the most weight. A total for the season is fine as a headline, but the breakdown is what makes it believable.

### Participation

Next, show what fans did with the sponsor's activation. Entries, redemptions, sign ups and scans all count. These numbers matter because they move past exposure into action, and they are the bridge between "people saw it" and "people responded to it."

### Audience profile

Then describe who those fans were, using shares. Season ticket holders, families, home and away supporters, age bands and regions are the usual cuts. Use bands rather than exact ages, and regions rather than postcodes. The sponsor needs the shape of the audience, not its fingerprints.

### Change over time

Finally, show whether the sponsor's audience grew or deepened across the season. Did more families attend the sponsor's matchday? Did repeat participation rise? This is the part that supports the renewal, because it shows the partnership is going somewhere.

## Privacy rules to follow when you share fan insights

Aggregate, suppress small groups, and write down what you share and why. This is general guidance, not legal advice, so check the details with your own privacy or legal lead before you change how you report.

### Aggregation is not automatically anonymous

The UK ICO is clear that data is only anonymous when people are no longer identifiable. Its [introduction to anonymisation](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/data-sharing/anonymisation/introduction-to-anonymisation/) also notes that creating aggregate statistics from personal data is itself processing, which needs a lawful basis and a clear purpose. So the act of building the sponsor's audience table sits inside your privacy obligations, not outside them.

### Watch small numbers

A table can identify someone even without a name on it. The [UK Data Service guidance on indirect identifiers](https://ukdataservice.ac.uk/learning-hub/data-producer-support/preparing-data-for-sharing-and-reuse/anonymising-data-and-protecting-participants/considerationsforcommonindirectidentifiers/) explains how combinations of age, location and other details can point to a person, and recommends banding, suppression and top coding.

In practice, set a minimum cell size for any segment you show, and merge or hide anything below it. The exact threshold is a decision for your privacy team, so agree it once and apply it everywhere.

### Put it in writing

The ICO [data sharing code of practice](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/data-sharing/data-sharing-a-code-of-practice) expects written agreements and clear authority when data moves between organisations. For sponsors, the easiest moment to settle this is at signing. Agree what audience reporting they get, at what level of detail, and how often.

### When the sponsor needs a match

Sometimes a sponsor wants to know how your audience overlaps with its own customers, or whether its sales moved. That is where a data clean room helps. Both sides compare first party data inside a secure environment and get back only aggregated results.

According to Forrester, as [cited by Cognizant](https://www.cognizant.com/uk/en/insights/blog/articles/data-clean-rooms-driving-high-performing-sports-partnerships-), 90% of respondents to its Marketing Pulse Survey 2024 were using clean rooms for marketing. [InfoSum](https://www.infosum.com/blog/how-sports-teams-and-leagues-can-dominate-the-field-with-data-collaboration) and [Sportradar's FanID](https://sportradar.com/content-hub/news/sportradar-launches-fanid-connecting-rightsholders-and-brands-with-sports-fans-in-a-post-cookie-world/?lang=en-us) are examples aimed at sports. A clean room is an option, not a requirement. ## How sponsors actually use the audience data you report

Sponsors use it to justify spend internally, to target their activations, and to decide whether to renew or grow.

The first use is the one you see least. Your contact has a finance team and a boss, and the audience section of your recap is often what they forward. If it is clear, sourced and easy to repeat, it does their selling for them. If you want the wider frame for that conversation, see how to [measure sponsorship ROI](/blog/how-to-measure-sponsorship-roi).

The second use is targeting. BCG's [Beyond Media Rights](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/beyond-media-rights-a-whole-new-ballgame-for-sports) describes how the Philadelphia 76ers ran more than 125 targeted fan campaigns in a year, lifted participation by about 15%, and used those activations to support the sale of a multiyear presenting sponsorship. The audience insight did not stay in a report. It shaped what the partner bought next.

The third use is the matching work clean rooms enable. [Navigate](https://nvgt.com/blog/data-clean-rooms-in-sponsorship-maximizing-effectiveness-securely/) and Cognizant both describe use cases such as audience overlap, customer acquisition and shifts in loyalty. These come from vendor and consultancy sources, so treat them as directional rather than benchmarks.

## Make the audience story specific to each partner

The same aggregated data can serve every partner, but each one needs its own cut. An automaker, a bank and a beverage brand care about different segments, and a generic audience slide tells all three that you were not really thinking about them.

An automaker may want to see the adult share of matchday crowds and the regions they travel from. A bank may care about season ticket holders and families. A beverage brand may care about attendance at specific events and redemptions of an offer. The underlying tables are the same. What changes is what you lead with.

## A simple fan data section for your sponsor recap

Drop this outline into your next recap. It works as a section inside a [post event sponsorship report](/blog/post-event-sponsorship-report), an end of season recap or a [sponsorship fulfillment report](/blog/sponsorship-fulfillment-report).

1. **Reach at sponsor moments.** Attendance and digital reach, by event or flight.
2. **Participation.** Entries, redemptions and sign ups tied to the activation.
3. **Audience profile.** Shares and bands, such as season ticket holders, families, age bands and regions.
4. **Change over the season.** What grew, what deepened, what dipped.
5. **Coverage.** What share of your total audience the data describes.
6. **Method and sources.** Where each number came from and when it was pulled.
7. **What was not shared and why.** A line on aggregation, suppression and your minimum cell size.

That last item sounds like a disclaimer, but it reads as confidence. It tells the sponsor you take your fans seriously, which is part of what they are paying to be associated with.
