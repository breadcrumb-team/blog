A **sponsorship fulfillment report** is the document rights holders send after a deal runs that proves every contracted asset was delivered, shows the evidence, and opens the door to renewal. It is not a photo dump. It is not a thank you PDF. It is the audit trail a brand champion can forward to finance with a straight face.

If you sell sponsorships, this report sits between signed contract and next year ask. Discovery tools help you find partners. Deal systems help you track inventory. The fulfillment report is where you close the loop with proof.

## What a sponsorship fulfillment report actually is

Think of it as four moves in one package:

1. **Contract** — what you promised, line by line
2. **Delivered** — what actually shipped, with honest status
3. **Proof** — photos, screenshots, counts, and notes that back each line
4. **Renewal ask** — what to keep, fix, or grow next season

Miss any of those four and sponsors fill the gap with their own story. That story is rarely kind to your renewal.

This is different from a full ROI essay. ROI asks whether the spend worked for the brand. Fulfillment asks whether you did what you sold. Both matter. Start with fulfillment, then connect outcomes. ## Why rights holders should own this report

Brands get pitched measurement tools built for their side of the table. Your job is different. You need a clear answer to: did we deliver the package, can we prove it, and what should next year look like?

Own the fulfillment report and you:

- Reduce "we never got X" surprises in renewal meetings
- Give your champion a file they can defend internally
- Surface over delivery so you can price and package smarter
- Catch misses early enough to offer make goods while goodwill remains

Leave it late, thin, or photo heavy with no asset table, and you hand the narrative to whoever has the loudest slide deck.

## Sponsorship fulfillment report template

Use this as a reusable shell. Copy it into a doc or deck and fill it per sponsor. Keep it short enough that a busy marketer finishes it in one sitting.

### 1. Cover

Property name, opportunity or season, dates, sponsor name and logo, your logo. Put their name on the cover so it feels built for them, not reprinted from a drawer.

### 2. Snapshot

Five to eight lines max:

- What ran (event, season, platform)
- Who showed up versus who you promised (audience actuals, not proposal projections)
- One sentence on overall delivery rate (for example, 42 of 44 contracted assets delivered)
- One sentence on the big win and one on the honest miss

### 3. Delivery chart (this is the report)

One row per contracted asset. Columns that work in practice:

| Asset | Status | Proof | Charged value | Realized value | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LED ribbon, home games | Delivered | Broadcast clip log | $18,000 | $21,400 | Over delivery on playoff window |
| Social package, 8 posts | Delivered | Post URLs + reach export | $6,000 | $5,800 | Two posts late; make good offered |
| Suite hospitality, 4 nights | Partial | Guest list + photos | $12,000 | $9,000 | One night weather cancel; credit issued |

Status should be plain: delivered, partial, missed, or over delivered. Notes should name the fix, not hide the miss.

The charged versus realized columns are where renewals get easier. A sponsor who can see value beside price does not need a speech.

### 4. Proof appendix

Photos and screenshots support the chart. They never replace it. Group proof by asset family (on site, broadcast, social, digital, hospitality, community). Caption each item with the asset name and date.

### 5. Outcomes and next ask

Separate what you can prove from what you can only estimate:

- **Prove:** delivery counts, reach where you have logs, redemptions, attendance tied to codes
- **Estimate carefully:** influenced pipeline, brand lift, "media value" theater without a method

Close with three bullets: keep, change, grow. Then propose a short call. The report earns the meeting. The meeting earns the renewal.

## Annotated example: mid tier season partner

Here is a compact story you can adapt. Names are illustrative.

**Sponsor:** regional bank, season partner  
**Fee:** $85,000  
**Window:** full home season

**Contract highlights**

- Sideline LED presence every home game
- Eight owned social posts plus four story sets
- Logo on digital ticket email footer
- Four suite nights with meet and greet
- Promo code for ticket offer, tracked in ticketing

**What the fulfillment report showed**

- LED and email footer: fully delivered, proof logged weekly
- Social: eight of eight posts live; reach slightly under plan on two midweek posts; make good of one bonus reel already scheduled
- Suite nights: three of four completed; one cancel for weather; credit applied toward a community clinic the bank already wanted
- Promo code: 1,140 redemptions; 62% first time buyers for the club

**Renewal ask that followed**

Lead with audience and redemption, not logo seconds. Propose keeping LED and email, growing the ticket offer, and swapping one underperforming midweek post slot for a creator co shot the bank liked in the proof appendix. The delivery chart made the miss and the make good visible, so the conversation stayed about next year instead of trust.

That is the point of a strong sponsorship fulfillment report. Honesty plus structure beats a glossy recap with no ledger.

## Build checklist before you hit send

Use this the day you think the report is done:

- [ ] Every contracted asset has its own row
- [ ] Every row has a status and a note
- [ ] Proof exists for delivered and over delivered lines
- [ ] Misses name a make good or credit
- [ ] Audience and delivery numbers are actuals, not proposal copy
- [ ] Charged and realized values are filled or clearly marked pending
- [ ] Keep / change / grow bullets are written
- [ ] A meeting ask is on the last page
- [ ] The file lands while the season or event is still warm (days, not weeks)

If your photos outnumber accounted assets, you built a recap. Go back and finish the audit.
